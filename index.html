Clara first noticed the problem not in her relationship, but in the data. Of the two hundred couples in Spectra’s beta test group, 87% showed asymmetrical conversational density — one partner consistently transmitting rich emotional signals, the other responding with emotional data that barely registered above silence.

The AI called it a “coherence gap.” Clara didn’t know what to call it yet. A modelling error, maybe. Overfitting? But the pattern was too consistent — and too familiar — to ignore. Spectra was supposed to help couples improve. That was the pitch. Upload your texts, voice notes, emails, conversations and let the AI tell you where communication breaks down — where you're misunderstood, where the nuance slips. Instead, the AI kept saying the same thing. Over and over. In hundreds of relationships. “One-sided signal detected.”

She hadn’t meant to run her own messages through the system. But it was late, she was alone in the house, and Eli had replied to her last message about their weekend plans with: “Up to you :)”

The app showed a dual-axis perplexity chart, each partner’s message entropy plotted across time. Clara’s line fluctuated with semantic variation and emotional density: dynamic, responsive, spiking around key exchanges. Eli’s began with modest variation in the early months, then declined steadily — eventually falling off like a cliff. The discrepancy became most pronounced near the end: a widening gap in communicative complexity, terminating in a message the model flagged as the final terminal signal drop.

Later that night, they watched a dating show — one of those later seasons where everyone was now too media-trained to be interesting. Eli stretched out on the couch, scrolling his phone. Clara at the opposite end. On-screen, a contestant was crying about someone’s head being turned. The others huddled around her, muttering about loyalty and intentions.

Clara glanced sideways. Eli didn’t react. “You still watching?” she asked.

“Yeah. Just reading.”

She nodded, though he didn’t look up. The episode ended with a montage: evening, someone sitting alone on a bench, voiceover lines about the sun setting on another relationship.

She wanted to ask something — not about the app, exactly, but something.

“You okay?” he asked eventually, still scrolling.

“Yeah. Just tired.”

They went to bed. Eventually, she placed her fingers lightly on the small of his back. He rolled onto his side, his body angling gently away from hers.

*

The next day the boardroom was quiet. Not the quiet of calm but of anticipation. The app had generated hype— features, interviews, a few glowing predictions — but Priyanka, the CEO, had made it clear: this meeting mattered. The data had taken months to gather, the modelling had ballooned beyond initial estimates, and they were running out of money for UI. If they couldn’t secure another round, Spectra wouldn’t launch at all.

Clara opened the beta results for the RSC algorithm on the screen behind her.

Seventy-three couples. One hundred and forty-six individuals. Perplexity data visualised as mirrored lines — one for each partner in a conversation.

They were supposed to be somewhat parallel. They almost never were.

“So,” Clara said, steady, “what you’re looking at is our first clean dataset.” She tapped the remote. The screen changed. “Here we’ve broken down ninety days of perplexity data into four aggregated measures. Together they produce the Relational Signal Coherence score. The vertical drop you’re seeing here—” she pointed “—represents partner two in each pair.”

There was a short pause.

Dan, the product manager, leaned forward. “So… partner two is consistently under-communicating?”

Clara nodded. “Yes. But not just frequency. It’s not about how much they say — it’s about what the language carries. Narrative follow-through. Curiosity markers…”

A few more nods. One person started typing.

Lara, an angel investor from Sydney, interjected. “Can I just… ask something maybe obvious?”

Clara paused. “Sure.”

“These are anonymised, right? But the gender pairing — it’s heterosexual?”

“Yes.”

“And… in how many cases is partner two the man?”

The room shifted slightly. A subtle tightening.

Clara glanced at her notes, though she already knew the number.

“Eighty-nine percent.”

Someone at the far end exhaled through their nose — not a laugh, but close.

Lara blinked. “Right.”

Another screen loaded. A line chart — jagged on one side, flat on the other.

“You’re saying,” Lara continued, “that in the majority of cases, women are emotionally broadcasting, as you say, and men are barely receiving?”

She had anticipated this. “I’m saying that the data suggests a consistent asymmetry in emotional signal,” Clara said, keeping her voice neutral. “Across written and verbal formats.”

Dan adjusted in his seat, angling towards Lara. “Okay but… that doesn’t mean people aren’t trying. Right? I mean, it could be language preference, or emotional style.”

“Of course,” Clara said. “That’s why it’s not failure. Just… disparity.”

She knew these were the wrong words.

Dan interjected this time. “Okay, so we soften the language. I mean, ‘asymmetry’ is clinical, sure, but could we reframe it? Make it more about styles, or preferences?”

“Or add additional measures,” Lara now offered. “Cognitive compatibility scores. Attachment styles. Something that balances out the algorithm.”

“You want to obscure it?” Clara asked.

“No, I want it to survive,” Lara replied. “We need adoption, not a reckoning.”

Another beat of silence.

Priyanka now closed her laptop softly. “We marketed this to women. If it’s going to work, it must feel constructive — not prescriptive. This narrative…” she gestured at the screen, “how does this help women?”

Clara looked at the chart again. In almost every instance the teal line climbed, curved, dropped. The grey one plummeted and then never moved.

*

On their first date, Eli took Clara to a local wildflower conservation reserve. She learned to tell a Grey Box from a Yellow Gum by the texture of the bark and shape of the leaves, the differences between biozones and EVCs, and that he really wanted to work in grassland rehabilitation — “the most neglected ecosystems,” he said: flat, unphotogenic, “never a funding priority.”

Not long after, he cleared space on his bedside table for her books and chargers. They started with arthouse films, then drifted into reality TV. Often, they sat at opposite ends of the couch, laptops open, reading aloud lines from articles about ecology or Tech.

They stayed in. Cooked. Let the television play.

It became routine. It felt like comfort. There was no real conversation, but no avoidance either. It just unfolded naturally around her commute, his work, the flat quietness of a small town after 8 p.m.

At some point, “we” started appearing in her sentences — casually, without much thought.
By the time she noticed, it was already embedded.

*

Clara didn’t go home that night. After the meeting she stayed in the office, until the blue cast of her monitor was the only light in the room. The train would come and go without her. She booked a hotel instead.

The outcome was clear: a complete product overhaul. Spectra would be rebranded as Perplex. The data viz of communication drift would be scrapped. In its place: one headline metric per couple, quantifying their communication alignment. Not what was said, but how well they matched. Individual scores wouldn’t be shown. Instead, couples would receive qualitative insights — assessments of “communication styles,” framed as normal miscommunication missteps. Behavioural experts would offer guidance: empathy, active listening, patience, kindness. The goal wasn’t reflection. It was optimisation. Progress, measured in incremental percentile shifts towards relational alignment.

Clara reopened the original Spectra dashboard — not the polished interface, but the raw logs.
Relationship timelines, message by message. She scrolled. One couple. Then another. Then another. Each thread playing out like a slow heartbeat. And then, inevitably, a flattening.

In most, the pattern was subtle: A drop in linguistic range. A missing “hey.” Fewer questions. Shorter replies.
“Sure.”
“Up to you.”
“No worries.”

She hovered over her own thread. It wasn’t flagged — anonymised like the rest — but she recognised the shape. A long teal curve. A quiet, uncurious grey.

“We’re probably the best communicators I know.”

She sat back. Considered, briefly, whether the narrative she presented was wrong.
How could a trail of semantic variation, plotted and colour-coded, reflect real emotional connection? Was I love you more powerful — more real — only with a timestamp?

A model knew nothing about silences that held meaning. Sunday mornings where nothing was said, but everything felt close. The texts he sent before driving back to town: Need anything from the supermarket? The glass of water he always left by her bed at night. The way he held her just a little too tightly each time he came back from the field. There was no metric for that. No variable for that kind of care.

She stared at the curve for what felt like hours — so neat, so decisive — a rendering of something that hadn’t felt neat at all. Just slow. And quiet. And maybe — after two years — done.

Abstraction blurs the details but sharpens the outline. And from this distance, the shape felt unmistakable. She took a screenshot and saved it to her desktop: spectra_final_clara_doubt.png.

*

Eli ended it over the phone the day of her first IVF consult. He’d stayed up north for an extra week, something about a delayed contractor or weather conditions, she wasn’t sure. They hadn’t spoken much — just the usual text messages: “Still raining,” “Long day,” “Speak soon.”

Then, Monday, after work, he called. “I’ve been thinking,” he said. “About everything. About us.”

She already knew.

He talked about the future. The climate. How he couldn’t, in good conscience, commit to building a shared life in a world that was collapsing. How he respected her choices, her independence — but that he just couldn’t align with a version of life that might include children. That it felt irresponsible. Ethically impossible. Politically… incoherent.

He didn’t cry, but he didn’t sound relieved, either. More like he was explaining a policy decision to a colleague. Clara stood by the window while he spoke, tracing a long flat line in the condensation on the glass.

In the background, a chainsaw whined. Wind caught the mic. His voice blurred in and out.

When he stopped talking, there was a pause — long, heavy.

“Hello? Can you hear me?” he asked.

“I can hear you…”

“…I didn’t want to do this over the phone...”

Another pause.

“Okay,” she said.

He waited. “You’re not going to say anything?”

The channel was noisy, but the message clear. She imagined her own soft teal line plummeting.

“No… not really.”

She ended the call. She didn’t cry. Nothing shattered. It was a Monday. Quiet. Grey. Clara opened her phone and deleted his messages and contact information.

*

On launch day, the tagline read: “Perplex: Because you both deserve to be understood.”

The antidote to all the glowing, glassy-eyed “AI Girlfriends” delivering pixel-perfect affection for $4.99 a month. This wasn’t fantasy or convenience. It was for real relationships. For depth. For couples trying to close emotional gaps through insight and repair. The app promised to correct miscommunication through machine learning, using gamified CBT-style strategies to help couples improve their “alignment” score over time. Wired called it “the first emotionally intelligent app that prioritises both voices in a relationship.”

*

Two weeks later, Clara pushed the original Spectra build to an anonymised GitHub repository under an MIT license. No announcement. No branding. Just a note in the README:

“Not a diagnostic tool.
Just a pattern visualiser.

Perplex was built to fix relationships.
Spectra was built to witness them.

Perplex tells you repair is always possible — if you're more attentive, more empathic…

Spectra doesn’t promise that.
It doesn’t interpret. It doesn’t advise.
It simply says: This is the shape your relationship took and this is when the signals stopped matching.

Built from silence.
Released for peace.”

By morning, it had a hundred forks and by the weekend, it was trending. Women began uploading chat logs, voice messages, emails, texts and watching their relationships bloom and break in colour-coded arcs. Some used it to make peace with past connections. Others watched present one’s dissolve in real time.

A whole genre of op-ed essays was born:

“Here’s where I started lying to myself.”

“I thought we were still building something. Turns out he’d already left.”
“This gave me the language to end it kindly…”

Journalists reached out, suspecting a leak from inside Perplex. A reporter from The Guardian approached Clara for comment on an article they’d titled, “Spectra: The AI App That’s Ending Relationships by Telling Women What They Already Knew.” They wanted her opinion on a specific screenshot that was circulating on Twitter X: a visualisation from Spectra of a text thread from the end of an eight-year marriage.

Emma: “I love you. Please let me know what I can do to help.”
Darren: “Sounds good. Night x”
Emma: 7.9
Darren: 0.4

The post had 83,000 likes.

Clara scrolled past the graph. Past the other tweets calling Spectra everything from emotional spyware to the breakup oracle. Someone had already forked it into a Chrome plugin called Closure. Someone else was pitching predictive overlays — neural nets trained to forecast “emotional ROI.” She closed the browser.

That morning, the clinic had called. The IVF round hadn’t worked. The nurse’s voice was gentle, practised. Reassuring. There was talk of next cycles, improved odds, new algorithms to optimise selection.

In the waiting room, she’d read an article in the company’s branded magazine, “The Future of Fertility Is Machine-Augmented.” Startups promising smarter embryo grading. Transformer models trained on temperament, viability, relationship stability. Some even claimed emotional metadata could improve implantation rates. She thanked the nurse. Hung up.

And in that moment, she thought of Eli. Just a flicker. A version of their life where their perplexity scores aligned. A version where it almost might have worked — where self-abandonment passed for compatibility, buffered instead by small kindnesses and familiar routines.

It passed. Only a data-shaped illusion.

There were no more dashboards now. No more metrics. No one left to optimise for. Some things too quiet to quantify, too fluid to graph. The kind of knowing that moves without signal.
